# Evidence for the reopened fading ticket: step checks, plan checks and the direction check

Desk research, 2026-10-03, for the reopened [How does the guidance fade across the problem set?](../issues/08-guidance-fading.md) and the alternatives in [fading-revisit-proposal.md](fading-revisit-proposal.md). It fills gaps in [fading-evidence.md](fading-evidence.md), [teaching-methods.md](teaching-methods.md) and [fading-apps.md](fading-apps.md) and does not repeat them.

**Tags.** **[V]** read in the source ("abstract" if only the abstract). **[S]** seen only in a search-result summary or a citing paper. **[I]** my inference.

## TL;DR

1. **Keeping step *checks* to the end shows no harm to unaided work. Keeping step *prompts* might.** Tutors that check every step beat no tutoring on post-tests by d = 0.76. Across 144 scaffolding studies, fading made no measurable difference. Trials with children split both ways. Fading worked best when someone kept checking the learner afterwards. **Strength: moderate for checks, weak and mixed for prompts.** *For the PoC: supports B, which fades what the app tells her and keeps what it checks. Section 3, Q6: yes. Q4: an unaided last try is worth adding as an option.*
2. **Check the plan line by line while plans are new to her. Once she gets lines right, let her check her whole plan herself before the app does.** Immediate feedback helped children and novices most. Letting learners catch their own errors first won in one adult study. **Strength: moderate for immediate feedback, weak for the delayed variant.** *For the PoC: supports Q3's recommended answer, plus a self-check before the app's line check from 4.1, switched on success.*
3. **Writing her own plan with checks probably beats being told each action, with the right dose of guidance.** Self-made subgoals helped more with hints *or* feedback, not both. Mostly adult evidence. **Strength: weak.** *For the PoC: supports Q2 (she writes the plan) and B over C and over "Level 2's mechanic to the end".*
4. **Memorising the routine helps but fades without review, and a long routine was too much for some 6th graders.** No trial tests "choose the next step" as the last fade. **Strength: weak.** *For the PoC: supports Q5 (she picks the next step), started earlier than 4.6 and repeated.*
5. **Children pick × or : by the size they expect the answer to be.** That gives the right sign in 2.3 and the wrong one when the multiplier is below 1 (дріб від числа). No trial shows a "more or less?" question improves operation choice. **Strength: strong that the habit exists, weak for the check.** *For the PoC: keep it in Levels 1–2 with its reason. On paper, make it a check of her result. Leave it off multipliers below 1.*
6. **Hold support while she struggles, then fade it per step.** Adaptive fading beat fixed fading, which did no better than none. **Strength: moderate.** *For the PoC: supports "fade a step after two right" over a fixed schedule, at least for the checks, which cost little data.*

## 1. Keeping step checks to the end

- **VanLehn 2011** [V]. Review of STEM tutoring experiments, 1975–2010, mostly high school and college. Against no tutoring: answer-based systems d = 0.31 (165 comparisons), step-based d = 0.76 (28), human tutors d = 0.79 (10). Step-based against answer-based: 0.40 (2). [doi:10.1080/00461520.2011.611369](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369). The post-tests were taken without the tutor [I].
- **Corbett & Anderson 2001** [V abstract], details from **Koedinger & Aleven 2007** [V]. Lisp tutor, college students. Immediate step feedback, error flagging and feedback on demand gave equal test results. All three beat feedback only at the end of each exercise, and immediate feedback was about three times faster. [doi:10.1145/365024.365111](https://doi.org/10.1145/365024.365111) · [doi:10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0)
- **Belland et al. 2017** [V abstract]. 144 studies of computer scaffolds in problem-centred STEM, primary to adult: g = 0.46. The effect "did not vary on the basis of … presence or absence of scaffolding change" (fading or adding). [doi:10.3102/0034654316670999](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316670999)
- **McNeill et al. 2006** [V abstract]. 331 7th graders, 8 weeks, written prompts for science explanations. On unprompted post-test items, the faded group reasoned better than the continuous group. [doi:10.1207/s15327809jls1502_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1502_1)
- **Bulu & Pedersen 2010** [V abstract]. 6th graders. Continuous domain-specific prompts beat faded ones on a content post-test. Faded general prompts helped problem-solving transfer. [doi:10.1007/s11423-010-9150-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-010-9150-9)
- **Wecker & Fischer 2011** [S]. 126 participants, argumentation scripts. Learners may "relapse into novice strategies" once prompts fade. A faded script plus a peer monitoring the strategy beat either alone. [doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.05.001)
- **Self-regulation prompts.** Metacognitive prompts: g = 0.40 on learning, larger when adaptive and with feedback (Guo 2022 [V abstract], [doi:10.1111/jcal.12650](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12650)). In a later session without prompts, effects were unclear (Engelmann, Bannert & Melzner 2021 [V abstract], [doi:10.1186/s41039-021-00148-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41039-021-00148-w)).
- **Theory.** Fading is part of what makes support a scaffold, and software often leaves it out (Puntambekar & Hübscher 2005 [V abstract], [doi:10.1207/s15326985ep4001_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4001_1); Pea 2004 [S], [doi:10.1207/s15327809jls1303_6](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1303_6)). An argument, not data.
- **Expertise reversal** (Tetzlaff et al. 2025 [V abstract], 60 studies). Novices learn more with high assistance (d = 0.51), knowledgeable learners with low assistance (d = −0.43). Helping novices matters more than withholding help from experts. Less clear for younger students. [doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142)
- **Waiting for the tutor.** "Gaming the system" (fast guessing, clicking through hints) predicted post-test scores as strongly as prior knowledge. The authors suspect learned helplessness or performance orientation (Baker et al. 2004 [V abstract], middle-school classes [S], [doi:10.1145/985692.985741](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985741)). Feedback on help use changed help use but not learning (Aleven et al. 2016 [S], [doi:10.1007/s40593-015-0089-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-015-0089-1)).

**What it means** [I]. "Step support" mixes telling her what to do next (prompts) with checking what she did (checks). Step-level checking goes with better unaided tests, not worse. For prompts the evidence is mixed, and the child trial that measured unprompted work favoured fading. B fades prompts and keeps checks, which is Wecker & Fischer's winning arm. No study I found measured any harm from keeping checks to the end. The live risk is gaming: two-button checks invite guessing, so the records should flag fast wrong-then-right pairs. On Q7: step checks made practice faster, not slower (Corbett & Anderson).

## 2. Checking the plan line by line or after the whole plan

- **Kulik & Kulik 1988** [V abstract]. 53 studies. Classroom studies with real material usually favoured immediate feedback. Lab studies of test content favoured delayed. [doi:10.3102/00346543058001079](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001079)
- **Kandemir et al. 2026** [S]. 51 computer-based studies: no average difference between immediate and delayed feedback (g = 0.03). Educational level, domain and time limits moderated it. The directions weren't in what I could read. Delays were mostly between items, not between steps of one problem. [doi:10.1007/s10648-026-10117-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10117-8)
- **Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson 2016** [V]. 75 2nd graders, maths equivalence on computer. Next-day post-test: feedback after each problem 86%, after the whole set 78%, none 65%. Both kinds of feedback helped children with low prior knowledge. Neither helped those with high. Feedback after the set lowered children's self-ratings. [doi:10.1016/j.jecp.2016.03.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2016.03.009) · [full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED566264.pdf)
- **Mathan & Koedinger 2005** [V abstract], details via Koedinger & Aleven 2007 [V]. Excel tutor, adult spreadsheet novices [S]. The "intelligent novice" tutor let learners catch certain errors themselves when the formula's result looked wrong, with feedback only if they missed it. It beat immediate feedback on every measure, including transfer and retention a week later. The point: decide which errors the learner should catch herself, not how long to delay. [doi:10.1207/s15326985ep4004_7](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4004_7)
- **Razzaq & Heffernan 2009** [S]. ASSISTments, middle school: less proficient students gained most from step-by-step scaffolding, more proficient ones from hints on demand. [Heffernan's publications](https://www.neilheffernan.net/publications)

**What it means** [I]. While plans are new, a check after each line fits the child and novice data. As she gets lines right, Mathan's idea fits a whole-plan check: she writes the plan, answers one self-check that exposes a wrong plan («Остання дія знаходить те, що питають?»), then the app checks line by line. That is still immediate where it matters, before she computes on a wrong plan. Switch on success (§6), not on problem number.

## 3. Given subgoals or her own plan

- **Catrambone 1998** [V abstract]. Adults. Labels for groups of steps in worked examples helped learners form subgoals and solve new problems. Abstract labels beat surface ones. [doi:10.1037/0096-3445.127.4.355](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.127.4.355)
- **Margulieux & Catrambone 2019** [V abstract]. College programming. Learners who wrote their own subgoal explanations beat those given labels only when they got hints *or* feedback. With both, they did no better. Their conference version: "Providing too much guidance can be as detrimental as providing too little." [doi:10.1080/10508406.2018.1491852](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2018.1491852)
- **Morrison et al. 2015, 2016** [V abstract]. Intro programming. Given vs self-generated labels: mixed, then given labels won. [doi:10.1145/2787622.2787733](https://doi.org/10.1145/2787622.2787733) · [doi:10.1145/2839509.2844617](https://doi.org/10.1145/2839509.2844617)
- **Guan et al. 2022** [V abstract]. 130 Chinese children aged about 9, online maths examples. Subgoal labels did not raise scores. A version where children chose among labels improved near transfer. [doi:10.3389/feduc.2022.832901](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2022.832901)
- I found no trial with children comparing "the app names each action" with "the child writes the plan and the app checks it" [I]. The nearest is "you learn the steps that are faded" (fading-evidence §1).

**What it means** [I]. Plan cards and named actions are given subgoals: good for starting. Writing the plan teaches more, but only with one kind of guidance. B gives her a check after writing and no menu first, which is the "feedback only" arm. C (write, then pick from menus, with hints) is closest to the "both" arm that did no better. Her 2.3 slip fits Catrambone: she had the subgoal (60 : 20 = 3 рази) but not the method of the next one. A plan-line hint should name the relation, not only the quantity.

## 4. Handing over the routine

- **Solve It!** (Montague 2008 review [V], [PDF](https://meadowscenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/ldq-montague-winter08-11.pdf)). Students memorise the seven processes and their say/ask/check until they can recite them. Then come modelling and thinking aloud, and finally the student acts as "the teacher". Performance "declined over time without distributed review and practice". Two 6th graders did not reach the mastery criterion. The routine "may have been developmentally beyond their ability" and "should be modified for younger learners".
- **Montague 1992** [V abstract]. Six students aged 12–14 with learning disabilities improved, but "did not, however, maintain the strategy over time". [ERIC EJ445828](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ445828)
- **SRSD** (Case, Harris & Graham 1992, via Montague 2008 [V]). Four students in grades 5–6 with learning disabilities. One stage is "memorize the strategy steps", followed by independent performance. Performance on addition problems held after instruction.
- **Choosing the next step.** No trial found. The nearest: asking learners only for their *first step* correlated up to .92 with full-solution tests, in a quarter of the time (Kalyuga & Sweller 2004 [V abstract], grades 9–10, [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.558](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.558)).

**What it means** [I]. Recalling the routine's order is worth training, but eight steps is long for her age, and it decays without review. The next-step picker hands over the order and doubles as a first-step test the records can read. Starting it from 4.1 and repeating it fits the decay finding better than two problems at the end. If eight steps are too many, merge some (Знайти with Відомо), as the review advises for younger learners.

## 5. Direction checks and choosing the operation

- **Fischbein et al. 1985** [S]. 623 Italian pupils, grades 5, 7 and 9. Operation choice follows tacit models: multiplication as repeated addition (the multiplier must be whole), division as sharing. Decimal multipliers caused errors at every grade. [doi:10.2307/748969](https://doi.org/10.2307/748969)
- **Bell, Fischbein & Greer 1984** [S]. 12–13-year-olds: number size, problem structure and context all changed the operation chosen. [doi:10.1007/BF00305893](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00305893). Bell, Swan & Taylor 1981 [S]: the effect of multiplying and dividing by a number below 1 was a core difficulty. [doi:10.1007/BF00308139](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00308139)
- **De Corte, Verschaffel & Van Coillie 1988** [V abstract]. 116 Flemish 6th graders: the type of multiplier strongly affected operation choice. [PDF](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/bitstream/123456789/243352/2/infl_of_numbersize.pdf)
- **Graeber & Tirosh 1990** [S]. 4th and 5th graders already believe "multiplication always makes bigger". [doi:10.1007/BF00315945](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00315945)
- **Lortie-Forgues & Siegler 2017** [V abstract]. Most middle-school students mispredicted the direction of × and : with decimals below 1, even when they could compute them. Explanations naming both the operation and the numbers' sizes went with right answers. [doi:10.1037/edu0000148](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000148)
- **Lim 2011** [V abstract]. Prospective teachers (adults), two classes. Predicting "bigger or smaller" and voting before answering: gain of 1.3 SD against 0.87. [doi:10.1080/0020739X.2011.573873](https://doi.org/10.1080/0020739X.2011.573873)
- The estimate step in SBI's DISC and in Solve It! (teaching-methods.md) has not been tested on its own, as far as I found [I].

**What it means** [I]. In 2.3, "less than 3000" plus "3 times less time" points to : 3. That is the size habit working. The same habit gives the wrong sign in «дріб від числа» (3/5 of 40 is less than 40, yet it is 40 · 3/5) and with decimal prices. So:

- Keep the check before «у … разів» and rate actions in Levels 1–2, as built, with the reason shown («час у 3 рази менший, тож і відстань»), never "менше → ділимо".
- Leave it off actions whose multiplier is below 1, or ask it only after the action.
- When Обчисли goes to paper under B, the direction becomes the check of her typed result («Твій результат менший за 3000 м?»), later a self-question in the routine, like DISC's estimate. What fades is the app asking first, not the habit.
- Fade it per relation type after two right in a row. With two buttons a right answer is weak evidence, so count it only when the action that follows is right too.

## 6. A learner who struggles early

- **Salden et al. 2010** [V]. Geometry Cognitive Tutor. Lab: 57 German 9th and 10th graders. Classroom: 51 US students, with heavy attrition. Adaptive fading beat fixed fading and plain tutored practice on delayed tests (d = 0.59 lab, 0.74 classroom). Fixed fading did no better than practice. In the classroom, adaptive students got *more* worked steps early and fewer later. [doi:10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)
- **Kalyuga & Sweller 2005** [V abstract]. An algebra tutor adapted by quick tests beat yoked, non-adapted instruction. [doi:10.1007/BF02504800](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02504800)
- Tetzlaff et al. 2025 (§1): helping novices has the larger effect.

**What it means** [I]. Her 2.3 try says she is still a novice at choosing operations in Level 2. The evidence favours holding a step until she succeeds, then fading it per step. Holding B's checks (plan lines, results, direction) needs almost no extra data. Holding menus needs full menus for every problem that might be held.

## What the evidence argues against

- **Fading on a fixed schedule while she still fails.** Fixed fading did no better than none (Salden).
- **Checking only the final answer while she is new.** Slower than step checks (Corbett & Anderson); children gained less from end-of-set feedback (Fyfe).
- **"More or less" as a rule for the sign.** It is the documented error with multipliers below 1.
- **Every kind of help on one step at once** (menus, hints and feedback). Self-made subgoals lost their edge (Margulieux & Catrambone).
- **A long routine memorised once with no review.** It decayed (Montague).

## Limits

- No study compares keeping step checks to the end with removing them for 11-year-olds planning word problems. Prompt-dependence claims come from science explanations, argumentation and programming.
- Most tutor and subgoal studies used high-school or college students. The child studies are Fyfe (7–8 years old), Guan (about 9), Bulu and De Corte (6th grade), McNeill (7th grade) and Montague (12–14, with learning disabilities).
- Many sources were read only as abstracts or summaries. Kandemir et al.'s moderator directions are unknown to me.
- She is one learner. Group averages may not fit her.
