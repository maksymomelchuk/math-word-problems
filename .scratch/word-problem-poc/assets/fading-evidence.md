# Evidence on three fading questions

Written 2026-10-03 for the fading grilling ([fading-proposal.md](fading-proposal.md)). It builds on [teaching-methods.md](teaching-methods.md) and does not repeat it.

**Source marks.** *Abstract* / *full text*: read in this session. *Summary*: read only a summary in a citing paper or search result. *Memory*: not checked.

## TL;DR

1. **Short record: she writes it from Level 3.** Learners learn most about the steps that are faded, and producing beats recognizing. A 6th-grade trial found fading beat worked examples and plain practice. Keep "what is asked" and the decode prompted in Level 3, keep the model reveal, and bring the menus back after two "different" marks in a row. **Evidence: moderate for the principle, weak for this step at this age.**
2. **Diagram: she picks it from Level 3, and also sets what goes in it.** Choosing a diagram is a separate skill that students often lack, and teaching "which diagram suits which problem" helped 14-year-olds. But what predicts right answers is an *accurate* diagram: which bar is longer, where the «?» goes. Letting her set those is the bigger gain. No study compares "choose first" with "given". **Evidence: weak to moderate, indirect.**
3. **Self-check: keep the model, and replace the single "same / different" tap with 3–4 item checks tied to lines of the model.** Children, 4th–6th graders included, overrate their own answers. Seeing the correct answer helps; ticking it off item by item helps more. A format checklist *without* the model is the weakest option. **Evidence: moderate. Good child studies, but on recalled definitions, not maths write-ups.**
4. **Mistakes: keep hint-then-show inside a problem (B). Across problems, add a data-free version of C: after two misses in a row on the same step, the next problem shows that step's model part first, then fades it again.** Missed problems come back at most once, at the end of the level, judged by the notebook (a late, light A). No mastery gate (D). **Evidence: moderate for the in-problem feedback, weak to moderate for restoring support, weak for the replay choice.**

## 1. When does the short record move from menus to writing?

- **Renkl, Atkinson & Große 2004** (abstract; university students, from memory). Where the faded steps sat did not change outcomes. Learners learned most about **the principles whose steps were faded**. Fading also cut unproductive learning moments. The abstract confirms the "you learn the faded steps" claim. [doi:10.1023/B:TRUC.0000021815.74806.f6](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:TRUC.0000021815.74806.f6)
- **Renkl et al. 2002** (summarized in the full text of Atkinson et al. 2003). Fading beat example–problem pairs on near transfer but not far transfer. Errors made during learning partly explained the gain. Backward fading beat forward fading. [doi:10.1080/00220970209599510](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510)
- **Moreno, Reisslein & Delgoda 2006** (summary; engineering freshmen; conference paper). Forward fading and adaptive practice beat backward fading on near transfer. [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/toward-a-fundamental-understanding-of-worked-example-instruction-/)
- **Große 2015** (abstract; 8th graders, per teaching-methods.md). Example–problem pairs taught translating a situation into a maths model best. Backward fading taught calculation best. [doi:10.30935/scimath/9444](https://doi.org/10.30935/scimath/9444)
- **Miller-Cotto & Medrano 2025** (abstract; 114 6th graders, online geometry homework). Fading gave the largest pre–post gains, ahead of worked examples with self-explanation and plain practice. Prior knowledge was the only thing that changed how well fading worked. [doi:10.1111/bjep.12781](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12781)
- **Generation and retrieval.** Generating beats reading: d = 0.40 over 86 studies, mostly with adults and word lists (Bertsch et al. 2007, summary; [doi:10.3758/bf03193441](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03193441)). Practice tests beat restudy, g = 0.50, and recall practice beat recognition practice (Rowland 2014, summary; [doi:10.1037/a0037559](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)).
- **Where generating fails.** For complex material, studying examples beat generating. For simple material, generating won. With more expertise, generating won on both (Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2015, abstract; school students, geometry; [doi:10.1037/edu0000018](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000018)).
- **Adaptive fading** beat fixed fading, which beat plain problem solving, with high-school geometry students (Salden et al. 2010, summary; [doi:10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)).

**What it means.** The one robust result is "you learn what you are made to produce." The direction of fading is not robust: three studies give three answers. The short record is required on paper, so 15 production reps with a reveal beat 7. After about 15 full write-ups, writing «?» and the givens with units is simple material, where generating wins. The comparison line is the complex part, so the decode stays prompted. One wrinkle: the record contains the restated comparison. In Level 3, run the decode *before* she writes the record. The risk is Renkl 2002's error finding: fading hurts if she keeps getting the record wrong. Guard: two "different" marks in a row bring the menus back for one problem. **Strength: moderate for the principle, weak for this application.**

## 2. Should she choose the diagram before it is given?

- **Hegarty & Kozhevnikov 1999** (summary; 6th graders). Schematic drawings went with success and pictorial ones with failure. [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.91.4.684](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.4.684)
- **van Garderen 2006** (abstract; 66 6th graders, from learning-disabled to gifted). The same pattern. [doi:10.1177/00222194060390060201](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222194060390060201)
- **Boonen et al. 2014** (summary; Dutch upper-primary children). Inaccurate schematic drawings lowered the chance of success. [doi:10.1016/j.ijer.2014.08.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijer.2014.08.001). De Koning et al. 2022 (75 5th graders, teaching-methods.md §3) is the sharpest case: about 99% correct with accurate bars and about 1.4% with inaccurate ones.
- **Uesaka, Manalo & Ichikawa 2007** (summary; 614 Japanese and New Zealand students). Students who saw diagrams as "the teacher's tool", or lacked confidence with them, drew fewer. [doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2007.02.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2007.02.006)
- **Ayabe, Manalo & de Vries 2022** (full text; 67 Japanese students, about 14 years old). Three 45-minute lessons taught *which* diagram suits *which* problem: a line diagram for comparisons, a table for patterns, a graph for trajectories. Appropriate diagram use and correct answers rose (d = 0.88–1.55), and the gains held at a delayed test. Multiple-baseline design, no control group. [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.992625](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.992625)
- **Zahner & Corter 2010** (abstract; 34 adults, probability): drawings helped only when a suitable one was chosen. [doi:10.1080/10986061003654240](https://doi.org/10.1080/10986061003654240)
- **Fuchs et al. 2003 and 2004** (abstracts; 3rd graders; 16-week randomized trials, 375 students in 2003). Explicitly teaching students to recognize problem types despite surface changes improved near and far transfer, beyond solution instruction alone. [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.293](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.293) · [doi:10.3102/00028312041002419](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312041002419)
- **Schoenherr et al. 2024** (meta-analysis, abstract). Visualization interventions in maths: g = 0.50 across 41 studies. I could not see whether provided or self-made visuals did better. [doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2024.100639](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2024.100639)

**What it means.** In SBI, naming the type already picks the schema diagram. Picking the diagram adds something only when she must learn the mapping (several types share the bars) or fit two relations into one diagram. That mapping is what Ayabe taught, and she will need it on paper. So the evidence supports the alternative, and it costs one tap. But the stronger evidence is about accuracy *inside* the diagram. If the pre-drawn bars arrive with their lengths already right, the app does the very step that predicts success. **Strength: weak to moderate, indirect. No direct trial.**

## 3. Self-checks of work the app can't read

- **van Loon & Roebers 2017** (abstract; 4th and 6th graders). Children were highly confident whether or not they were right. Showing the ideas the answer should contain, either as a full answer or as a list of idea units, strongly improved how they judged wrong and partly right answers. [doi:10.1002/acp.3347](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3347)
- **Lipko et al. 2009** (abstract; middle school). With no standard, students gave credit to 73% of their wrong answers. With the correct definition shown, they credited 44%. Ticking each idea unit as present or absent cut overconfidence further. [doi:10.1037/a0017599](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017599)
- **For complex products the result flips.** When students judged examples they had made up, a full expert example helped and idea units did not (Froese & Roelle 2022, abstract, 131 university students, [doi:10.1007/s11409-022-09293-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-022-09293-z); Zamary et al. 2016, summary, [doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2016.08.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2016.08.002)).
- **Baars et al. 2014** (summary; secondary-school students, problem solving). Giving the correct solution as a standard improved self-assessment. [doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2014.04.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2014.04.004)
- **Raaijmakers et al. 2019** (abstract; secondary-school students). Telling students how accurate their self-assessments had been did not improve their later accuracy. [doi:10.1007/s11409-019-09189-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-019-09189-5)
- **Sadler & Good 2006** (abstract; 101 7th graders, science). Self-grading with a rubric matched the teacher closely (r = .91–.94), though low scorers inflated their grades. Students who graded their own tests improved a lot on a retest a week later; students who graded a peer's test did not. [doi:10.1207/s15326977ea1101_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326977ea1101_1)
- **Checklists and rubrics.** Self-assessment interventions had g = 0.59 on performance across 175 studies (Yan et al. 2022, abstract, [doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2022.100484](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2022.100484)). With secondary students, step-by-step self-assessment scripts and rubrics both beat a control; scripts did more for self-regulation (Panadero et al. 2012, summary, [doi:10.1016/j.lindif.2012.04.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2012.04.007)). Metacognitive self-questions improved 384 8th graders' written maths explanations (Kramarski & Mevarech 2003, abstract, [doi:10.3102/00028312040001281](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312040001281)).

**What it means.** Keep the full model: for complex products like a write-up, the full model is the standard that helped. But a single "same / different" tap is the weakest way to use it. Expect "same" for many records that differ. Add 3–4 item checks, each pointing at a line of the model:

- «Біля AC у тебе стоїть знак питання?»
- «Ти записала "AC на 5,1 більша за BC"?»
- «Після кожної дії є пояснення?»
- «Відповідь — повним реченням?»

Format-only items («Чи є знак питання?») can be ticked even when the content is wrong. Keep "correct it in the notebook": Sadler & Good suggest the correcting is where the learning happens. Use the typed final answer as the one objective anchor; a wrong answer with "same" deserves a gentle flag. Don't expect accuracy feedback on its own to calibrate her (Raaijmakers et al.). **Strength: moderate.**

## 4. Reacting to mistakes

- **Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen 2015** (abstract; 40 studies of computer-based feedback). Feedback that explains beat giving the correct answer, which beat right/wrong alone (0.49 vs 0.32 vs 0.05). Effects were larger in maths, and smaller with delayed feedback and in primary and high school. [doi:10.3102/0034654314564881](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881)
- **Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020** (abstract; 435 studies). Feedback: d = 0.48 overall. What it tells the learner matters most. [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087)
- **Shute 2008** (summary; review). Use immediate feedback for difficult tasks and weaker learners, and delayed feedback for simple tasks and strong learners. [doi:10.3102/0034654307313795](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795)
- **Metcalfe 2017** (abstract; review). Errors followed by corrective feedback help learning, most of all when the learner was confident. [doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022)
- **Attali 2015** (summary; maths problems). A second try after feedback improved later success on similar problems, more for typed answers than for multiple choice. [doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2015.08.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2015.08.011)
- **Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson 2016** (abstract; about 100 primary children per experiment, maths equivalence). Right/wrong feedback helped children who did not know a correct strategy. It hurt children who did. [doi:10.1037/edu0000053](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000053)
- **Sinha & Kapur 2021** (summary; 53 studies). Problem solving *before* instruction beat instruction-first on understanding and transfer (d = 0.36). Students from 6th grade up gained more than younger ones. This is about designed struggle before teaching, not about leaving practice errors without help. [doi:10.3102/00346543211019105](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- **Adaptive support.** Support adjusted to the learner beat fixed support: Salden et al. 2010 (§1), and Kalyuga & Sweller 2005 (summary; algebra tutor, learner-adapted vs yoked non-adapted; [doi:10.1007/bf02504800](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02504800)). Renkl 2002's error finding (§1) points the same way.
- **Spacing.** Spaced practice beats massed practice (Cepeda et al. 2006, abstract, 317 experiments; [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)). In maths, splitting 10 problems over two sessions helped a lot at 4 weeks. Six extra problems in one session did nothing (Rohrer & Taylor 2006, abstract, college; [doi:10.1002/acp.1266](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1266)).
- **Re-solving a seen problem.** After studying worked examples, solving the identical problem, or an isomorphic one, was no better than restudying (van Gog et al. 2015, abstract, four experiments; [doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9297-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9297-3)). Practice testing transfers least for worked-example problems (Pan & Rickard 2018, preprint abstract, d = 0.40 overall; [doi:10.1037/bul0000151](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)).
- **Mastery learning** raised exam scores, most for weaker students, but took more time (Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990, abstract, 108 studies; [doi:10.3102/00346543060002265](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265)). Both sides of the debate agree the effect on standardized tests is about zero (Slavin 1990, abstract; [doi:10.3102/00346543060002300](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002300)).
- **Criterion learning** ("recall it correctly 3 times"; Karpicke & Roediger 2008; Vaughn & Rawson 2011, summary) comes from word pairs and facts, not multi-step problems.

**What it means.**

- **Inside a problem, keep B.** The current pattern already matches the best-supported one. On prompted steps: a hint, then the answer with its reason. On paper: «Не сходиться», then the model, then "where did yours go off". That is one more try, then explaining feedback, given at once. Don't add unguided struggle time: productive failure is a way to design lessons, not a rule for practice.
- **Across problems, add a data-free C.** After two misses in a row on the same step (her "where it went off" tap or a "different" mark), the next problem shows that step's model part *first*, as a worked step. She writes the rest herself. The problem after that fades it again. This is adaptive fading with worked steps. It needs no menu data, because every problem has its write-up. It is also one readable log line for the parent. Keep 4.6–4.7 unaided so the ending stays a clean check.
- **A, but late and judged by the notebook.** Replaying the identical problem is a weak test, because she has seen the model and may remember the number. It is also modest practice (van Gog et al.). The next problem of the same type is the better "sister" retry, and the fixed set already supplies one. If replays are offered, put each missed problem once at the end of its level. The parent reads the notebook, not the number.
- **Not D.** A fixed 30-problem set has no fresh pool, so a gate would loop on problems she has already seen. That tests memory, not mastery, and costs real time.

**Strength: moderate for the in-problem feedback, weak to moderate for restoring support, weak for the replay choice.** No study tests these options with 11-year-olds on word-problem write-ups.

## What the evidence argues against

- **Fading several steps at once on the hardest problems.** In the current schedule, Level 4 is where the short record and the decode both become production, on chains. In fading-proposal 4.1–4.2, Знайти, the diagram and the plan switch too. The fading studies remove one more step per problem.
- **A pre-drawn diagram that encodes the decode.** If the bar lengths arrive already right, she never practises the accuracy that predicts success.
- **"Same / different" as the only self-check.** Without an item-by-item standard, children give their own work too much credit.

## Limits

No study tests these exact choices with 11-year-olds writing up word problems. Most fading studies used older learners and computer tutors. The self-assessment studies used recalled definitions or science tests, not maths on paper.
