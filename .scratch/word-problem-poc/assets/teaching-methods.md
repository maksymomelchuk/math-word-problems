# Word-problem teaching methods for 10–12-year-olds

**TL;DR.** The best-supported approach is a short, fixed routine: read and retell, find the question, list what is given, name the problem type, put the numbers into a ready-made diagram, plan the steps, compute, check. US practice guides rate its parts "strong evidence": self-monitoring prompts (WWC 2012) and problem-type diagrams (WWC 2012, WWC 2021). Schema-based instruction (SBI) has the best trials with typical 12–13-year-olds, though effects shrink on standardized tests. Pólya's phases are a good skeleton but weak on their own. Bar models suit "greater/less by" problems, but their evidence is mostly correlational. The "BC is *less* than AC by 5.1" trap is well documented at her age. Two things help: rebuilding the sentence ("who is bigger?", then flip it) and drawing an *accurate* diagram. Keywords make it worse. For carry-over to paper: fade worked-example steps one by one, mix the problem types late in the set, and remove the checklist at the end.

## At a glance

| Method | What the learner does | Evidence | Phone fit | Carries to paper? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pólya's four phases | Understand → plan → carry out → look back | **Weak** alone; **strong** as a prompt list (WWC Rec 2) | Good as step headers | Yes, as a checklist |
| Schema-based instruction (Jitendra) | Find type → organize numbers in a schema diagram → plan → solve/check | **Strong** for structured problems; less on standardized tests | Very good: tap type, drag numbers into pre-drawn diagram | Yes: type name + sketch |
| Singapore bar model | Draw bars for quantities and relations → read the operation off | **Weak–moderate** (descriptive, correlational) | Freehand awkward; pre-drawn resizable bars work | Yes: fast to draw |
| Worked examples with fading | Study example → complete partial examples → solve alone | **Moderate** | Excellent: fading = pre-filled steps | It is the route to unaided work |
| Fixes for inconsistent language | "Who is bigger?" → restate → check diagram | Trap: **strong**; fixes: **moderate** | Excellent: 2–3 taps | Yes: portable self-question |
| Solve It! (Montague) | 7 steps (read … check), each with say/ask/check | **Weak–moderate**: gains on program-aligned tests, none detected on a state test | Good | Yes, but long |
| Keyword strategy | "less → subtract" | Evidence **against** | n/a | Harmful |

## 1. Pólya's four phases (and the "task list" version)

**Steps.** Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) has four phases: (1) understand the problem: what is the unknown, what are the data, draw a figure; (2) devise a plan: have you seen a related problem?; (3) carry out the plan and check each step; (4) look back: check the result.

**Evidence: weak on its own, strong in operational form.** Schoenfeld found Pólya's heuristics "descriptive rather than prescriptive": you can recognize them when someone uses them, but not learn to use them from the description ([Schoenfeld 1992](https://jwilson.coe.uga.edu/emat7050/schoenfeld_maththinking.pdf)). In contrast, WWC rates "assist students in monitoring and reflecting on the problem-solving process" **strong**. That rating rests on 9 studies in grades 4–8, all positive, using short task lists or self-questions: "What is the problem asking?", "What information is given?", "Identify the problem type", "Check the solution" ([WWC 2012](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/16), App. D). Closest to her age: a Pólya-like 5-phase strategy with Belgian 5th graders gave ES = 0.31, and 0.38 on transfer ([Verschaffel et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327833mtl0103_2), via WWC Table D.2). Caveats: several studies bundled prompts with diagrams. The guide also warns that too many prompts slow students down and says to remove support gradually.

**Phone fit.** Good as fixed step headers ("1. Що питають? 2. Що дано? …") with tap answers.

**Paper.** Yes. A memorized 5–6 item checklist becomes headings on paper.

## 2. Schema-based instruction (Jitendra et al.)

**Steps.** For elementary problems the routine is FOPS: **F**ind the problem type, **O**rganize the information in the schema diagram, **P**lan, **S**olve ([Hott et al. 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1314840.pdf)). The middle-school ratio/proportion version is DISC: **D**iscover the problem type (read, retell, compare with known types), **I**dentify the information and put it in a diagram, **S**olve (estimate first, write the equation, choose a strategy), **C**heck ([Jitendra et al. 2015, manuscript](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED572835.pdf)). Schemas: *change*, *group/combine*, *compare*, *equal groups*, *multiplicative compare*, *ratio/proportion*. A problem type is **not** an operation: the same type can need different operations ([WWC 2021](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf), Rec 5).

**Evidence: strong for structured problems, with caveats.**
- Meta-analysis, 21 elementary studies (3,408 students, 324 with disabilities): g = 1.57 immediate, 1.09 transfer ([Peltier & Vannest 2017](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654317720163)). Treat these as upper bounds: the trials below show much smaller effects on independent tests.
- 148 typical 7th graders, 10 lessons: SBI beat controls at posttest and 4 months later, but **not on the state test** ([Jitendra et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2009.06.001)). WWC recalculated the effects as 0.33 and 0.38, not significant (WWC 2012, Table D.4).
- Cluster RCT, 1,999 7th graders: ES = 0.46 at posttest, 0.32 at 9 weeks, **no effect** on a standardized problem-solving subtest ([Jitendra et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000039); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1082754)).
- An evidence-based practice for students with or at risk for learning disabilities, based on group-design studies only ([Jitendra et al. 2015, JLD](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022219413487408)). Rated **strong** in WWC 2012 Rec 3 (visual representations) and WWC 2021 Rec 5 (instruction by problem type, struggling K–6 students).

So SBI works for typical learners too, but the gains are moderate and narrow: biggest on problems like the ones taught.

**Phone fit.** Very good. Tap one of 4–6 type icons. Drag the numbers and a "?" into a **pre-drawn** schema. Order 2–3 step cards. Use the same diagram for every problem of a type (WWC 2012 Rec 3).

**Paper.** Yes: write the type name and a quick sketch.

## 3. Singapore bar models (model method)

**Steps.** Ng & Lee's three phases are: (1) *text*: identify the quantities, the relations and the unknown; (2) *structural*: draw one rectangle per quantity, going back to the text for each relation; (3) *procedural-symbolic*: read the equation off the drawing ([Ng & Lee 2009](https://doi.org/10.5951/jresematheduc.40.3.0282); described in [de Koning et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10649-022-10150-8)). For "X is greater/less than Y by d": draw the known bar first, decide **which bar is longer**, then mark d as the extra piece.

**Evidence: weak–moderate.** WWC 2012 says "strip diagrams are suited for comparison and fraction problems", but that is panel judgment, not a bar-model trial. Ng & Lee (2009) is descriptive (151 Singapore Primary 5 children); errors often came from misrepresenting a single piece of information. [Morin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0731948717690116) is a small single-case study with students who have math difficulties. Most relevant for her: **75 Dutch 5th graders** solving consistent and inconsistent compare problems ([de Koning et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10649-022-10150-8)). Estimated chance of a correct answer on *inconsistent* problems:

| Diagram | Correct |
|---|---|
| Accurate bar diagram | ~99% |
| No drawing | ~23% |
| Inaccurate bar diagram | ~1.4% |

The authors call bar drawing a "double-edged sword": a wrong drawing locks in the wrong operation. The study is correlational, not randomized. I found no large RCT that isolates the bar model.

**Phone fit.** Freehand drawing is slow and imprecise. Cheap substitute: pre-drawn bars she **orders by length** (tap "which is longer") or **stretches** with a handle, then labels with dragged tags ("AB", "3.7", "?"). The app can **check the bar order before she computes**, which is the step the de Koning data says matters.

**Paper.** Yes: rough rectangles are the fastest diagram to draw.

## 4. Worked examples with fading (Renkl, Atkinson et al.)

**Steps.** (1) Study a fully worked example. (2) The next example has one step missing (usually the **last**, which is "backward fading"). (3) More steps are left blank. (4) Solve alone ([Renkl & Atkinson 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3); [Renkl et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510)). Prompts can ask the learner to explain each worked step, for example by choosing the principle behind it from a **menu** ([Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774); described in [Wylie & Chi](https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/wylie_chi_selfexplanation_0.pdf)).

**Evidence: moderate.**
- IES rates "interleave worked examples with problem-solving exercises" **moderate** ([Pashler et al. 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)).
- Meta-analysis across 55 math studies, from elementary to postsecondary: g = 0.48 ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1364058)). In that analysis, *adding* self-explanation prompts to examples was associated with **smaller** effects.
- The fading studies are small, with older learners: German 9th graders (physics) and US university students (probability). Fading improved near transfer. Self-explanation prompts added to backward fading also improved far transfer (Renkl & Atkinson 2003, Table 1); in Atkinson et al. 2003 the prompts were menu-based.
- With 8th graders, translating a situation into math improved most with plain example–problem pairs, and calculation with backward fading ([Große 2015](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1107878.pdf)).
- I found no fading study with 10–12-year-olds aimed at word-problem *planning*.

**Phone fit.** Excellent: fading just means "which steps arrive pre-filled", and menu prompts are taps.

**Paper.** This is the route to unaided paper work, but only if the last stage really is unaided.

## 5. The inconsistent-language trap

**The finding is robust.** People make more errors when the relational word clashes with the operation, as in "BC is *less* than AC by 5.1", where AC is unknown and you must add ([Lewis & Mayer 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.79.4.363); [Pape 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0361-476X%2802%2900046-2)). Successful solvers build a *problem model* of the situation; unsuccessful ones *translate directly* from numbers and keywords ([Hegarty, Mayer & Monk 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.87.1.18), undergraduates). Among **10–12-year-olds**, the "less than" form is the worst: less successful solvers erred on ~97.5% of these problems ([van der Schoot et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2008.07.002)). Her triangle problem has exactly this form.

**What helps:**
1. **Rebuild the relation sentence.** An RCT with 31 second-grade classrooms taught compare problems with explicit work on relational words. All compare subtypes improved, and the gain was fully or partly *explained by* better understanding of more/less/fewer ([Schumacher & Fuchs 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2011.12.001)). The techniques ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3288282/)):
   - cover the difference number and decide who is bigger;
   - swap subject and object and flip the word ("Tom has 5 fewer than Jill" → "Jill has 5 more than Tom");
   - Bigger − smaller = Difference.
2. **Diagram training.** [Lewis 1989](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.81.4.521) taught college students to place quantities on a number line. A later review reports that this cut reversal errors but did *not* raise overall scores ([Xin et al. 2005](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ722297.pdf)); I could not access the primary abstract. Accurate bar diagrams are also strongly linked to correct answers (section 3).
3. **Explicit warning.** A short verbal instruction about the trap reduced it in children ([de Koning et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2017.01.006)); grade and wording not verified.

**Phone fit.** Excellent: "Who is bigger, BC or AC?" (tap), then "AC is ___ than BC by 5.1" (pick a word), then an automatic bar-order check.

**Paper.** Yes: "Who is bigger? Flip the sentence" is a self-question she can take into the paper check.

## 6. Other approaches (brief)

- **Keyword strategy: avoid.** In 747 released test items for grades 3–8, keywords pointed to the right operation in <50% of single-step and <10% of multi-step problems ([Powell, Namkung & Lin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1086/717888)). The WWC 2021 panel advises against keywords. This is item analysis plus expert opinion, not an RCT.
- **Self-explanation prompts.** g = 0.55 across subjects ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)). In math the gain is small to moderate, measured immediately, and larger when explanations are scaffolded ([Rittle-Johnson et al. 2017](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1149060)). Use few, menu-based prompts (see the Barbieri caveat in section 4).
- **"What is asked / what is given."** This is part of the WWC Rec 2 task list. 4th graders did better with the question *before* the text, especially weaker students ([Thevenot et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210600587927)). Spotting irrelevant information can be taught ([Powell et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED598432.pdf)).
- **Solve It!** Seven steps, each with say/ask/check ([Özkubat et al. 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1262581.pdf)). An RCT with 1,059 7th graders found growth on a curriculum-based measure, a bit stronger for low achievers ([Montague et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035176)). WWC judged that measure too closely aligned with the program and found **no discernible effect** on the state test ([WWC review](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/SingleStudyReviews/wwc_montague_042914.pdf)).
- **Meta-analyses (students with math difficulties).** [Zheng, Flynn & Swanson 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0731948712444277): g = 0.95 (math difficulties only), −0.45 (with reading difficulties). [Myers et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211070049): 52 studies, g = 0.81 without outliers. I could not find "Zheng, Flores & Flores 2013"; it is probably this Zheng, Flynn & Swanson paper.
- **Interleaving.** Mixed instead of blocked practice: d = 0.83 on a delayed test in a cluster RCT with 7th graders ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)). Naming the problem type is only real practice when the lesson does not give the type away.

## Recommended step sequence for the PoC

One guided problem goes through these steps:

1. **Read and retell**: pick the best one-line retelling (Pólya "understand"; SBI "retell"; Solve It! "paraphrase").
2. **What is asked?** The unknown, with units (WWC Rec 2; Thevenot 2007).
3. **What is given?** Each number with label and unit; flag unit mismatches (WWC Rec 2).
4. **Decode relation sentences**: "Who is bigger?", then "bigger = smaller + d" (Schumacher & Fuchs 2012; Lewis 1989).
5. **Name the type, fill the model**: pre-drawn schema or bars (SBI; bar model; WWC Rec 3).
6. **Plan**: order step cards, saying what each finds (Pólya; SBI). The main target for fading.
7. **Estimate**: more or less than X? (DISC; Solve It!)
8. **Compute**: short typed input; her strength.
9. **Check**: against the model, the estimate and the units (Pólya "look back"; DISC).

**Triangle** (AB = 6 cm for illustration; "BC на 3,7 см більша за AB, але на 5,1 см менша від AC"):
1. Retell: one side known, two comparisons, find the perimeter.
2. Asked: P = AB + BC + AC, so all three sides are needed.
3. Given: AB = 6 cm, plus two relations.
4. Decode. "BC greater than AB by 3.7": BC = AB + 3.7. "BC less than AC by 5.1": who is bigger? **AC**. So "AC is greater than BC by 5.1", AC = BC + 5.1.
5. Model: compare ×2, then total. Bars AB < BC < AC, with extra pieces 3.7 and 5.1.
6. Plan: BC, then AC (it needs BC), then P.
7. Estimate: AC is the longest; P ≈ 3 × BC ≈ 30.
8. Compute: BC = 9.7, AC = 14.8, P = 30.5 cm.
9. Check: AC is the longest; AC − BC = 5.1; units cm.

**Walking boy** ("за 1 год проходить 3000 м; скільки за 20 хв?"):
1. Retell: same speed; distance per hour known; find the distance in 20 minutes.
2. Asked: distance in metres.
3. Given: 3000 m in 1 h; 20 min. **Unit mismatch: 1 h = 60 min.**
4. Decode: no compare sentence; the hidden relation is "same speed", which means a proportion.
5. Model: rate/proportion. Ratio table or double number line (60 min ↔ 3000 m, 20 min ↔ ?), or a 3000 m bar cut into 3 equal parts.
6. Plan: convert to minutes; 60 ÷ 20 = 3; 3000 ÷ 3. Or 3000 ÷ 60 = 50 m/min, then × 20.
7. Estimate: less than 3000, about a third.
8. Compute: 1000 m.
9. Check: 1000 × 3 = 3000; metres.

**Fading across a 20-problem set** (a suggestion, not a tested design):
- **A (problems 1–3):** fully worked examples, one per type, with 1–2 menu "why?" prompts at the decode and plan steps.
- **B (4–9):** completion problems with backward fading. Hide the last steps first (check, compute), then plan, then model, and decode last.
- **C (10–15):** only the step headings remain. Problem types are mixed, with at least 3 inconsistent compare items. Feedback only on request.
- **D (16–20):** blank problems done **on paper** with a printed checklist card, in the same layout (Given/Find, sketch, plan, answer). The app takes only the final answer and a self-check. The card goes away for the last 2–3 problems.

Optionally, fade a step only after she has done it correctly twice. That is a simple form of adaptive fading; I have not verified that it beats fixed fading.
